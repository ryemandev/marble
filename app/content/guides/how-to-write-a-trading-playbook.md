A trading playbook is a short document that defines every setup you're allowed to trade: when it's valid, what triggers the entry, where the stop goes, how you manage and exit it, and when to skip it. Each setup's rules are written so you can tick them yes or no before you enter, which turns "I think this is a good trade" into a checklist. Your journal then measures each setup separately, so the playbook keeps what works and shows you what doesn't.

## Why write a playbook?

- **It defines what a "good trade" is before the trade.** Without written rules, you judge trades by outcome, and outcome is partly luck.
- **It gives your journal categories.** Results by setup are only meaningful if each setup means the same thing every time.
- **It makes "no setup" visible.** Anything that doesn't fit the playbook is, by definition, unplanned. That's often where the losses hide.
- **It tells you what "on plan" means.** If the trade met the checklist, it was on plan, win or lose.

## What to include for each setup

| Section | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **Name** | A short, fixed name you'll use in your journal every time |
| **Market and timeframe** | Which contracts or symbols, and which chart you trade it from |
| **Context** | When the setup is valid: time window, market conditions you require |
| **Trigger** | The exact event that gets you in |
| **Stop** | Where the initial stop goes, and why that's where the idea is wrong |
| **Risk** | How many dollars you risk, and maximum size |
| **Management** | What you do once in: partials, moving the stop, adding |
| **Exit** | Target, time stop or other exit rule |
| **Skip conditions** | When you don't take it, even if it looks right |
| **Examples** | Two or three annotated screenshots: a clean one, a loser that followed the rules, and a near miss you skipped |
| **Numbers** | Trades, win rate, net P&L, average win and loss in dollars, updated from your journal |

## How to write rules you can check

The test of a good rule is whether two people looking at the same chart would answer it the same way. Compare:

| Vague | Checkable |
|---|---|
| "Strong trend" | "[Your trend definition] is true on the [timeframe] chart" |
| "Good entry" | "Entry is on [your trigger], not before it" |
| "Sensible stop" | "Stop is beyond [the level that proves the idea wrong]" |
| "Not during news" | "No entry from [X] minutes before to [Y] minutes after a scheduled release" |
| "Don't overtrade" | "No more than [N] trades of this setup per session" |

The brackets are yours to fill in; this guide doesn't tell you what to trade. The point is the form: each rule gets a yes or a no.

Keep it to a handful of rules per setup. Five you check every time beat twelve you skim.

## A fill-in playbook template

Copy this for each setup. The example wording shows format only; it isn't a setup to trade.

> **Setup name:** [e.g. "Morning pullback"]
>
> **Markets and timeframe:** [e.g. MNQ, MES; 5-minute chart]
>
> **Valid when:** [time window, in New York time if you trade US futures] · [market condition you require]
>
> **Rules (all must be yes):**
> 1. [Context rule]
> 2. [Trigger rule]
> 3. [Stop placement rule]
> 4. [Risk rule: dollars at risk per trade, maximum contracts]
> 5. [Skip rule: e.g. no entry close to a scheduled release]
>
> **Management:** [what you do at a set profit in dollars, if anything] · [when the stop moves, if ever]
>
> **Exit:** [target or exit rule] · [time stop, if any]
>
> **Plan mark:** every line ticked = on plan; any line missed = off plan, and note which one
>
> **Numbers (from the journal):** trades __ · win rate __ · net P&L $__ · average win $__ · average loss $__ · last reviewed __

## Your playbook's numbers

Once a setup has a name and rules, your journal can measure it:

- **Dollars made or lost,** and **expectancy** in dollars per trade, which tell you whether it has paid; see [what is expectancy in trading](/guides/what-is-expectancy-in-trading/).
- **Dollars on plan** against **dollars off plan**, which tells you whether the checklist is pulling its weight.
- **Which line gets missed most,** which tells you where the work is.

Note the number of trades next to every figure. Early numbers move a lot.

## Reviewing and changing the playbook

Look at the playbook as part of your [weekly review](/guides/how-to-review-your-trades-weekly/), and change it slowly:

1. **One change at a time,** dated, so you can compare before and after.
2. **Change on evidence,** not on one bad day. A setup that followed its rules and lost three times in a row may be fine.
3. **Retire a setup openly.** Move it to an "archived" section with its numbers, rather than deleting it.
4. **Mark new setups as "trial"** until they have enough trades to judge, so their early results don't blur the established ones.

## Common playbook mistakes

- **Too many setups.** Each one needs its own sample of trades. Spreading thin means none of them has enough data.
- **Rules that describe hindsight.** "It went straight up" isn't a rule. Rules must be checkable before you click.
- **No skip conditions.** Knowing when not to trade a setup is as important as the trigger.
- **Never updating the numbers.** A playbook without results is a wish list.
- **Treating "no setup" as normal.** If you often trade outside the playbook, that's a rule to write, or a habit to break; see [how to stop revenge trading](/guides/how-to-stop-revenge-trading/).
