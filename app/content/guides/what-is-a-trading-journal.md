A trading journal is a record of every trade you take: what you traded, where you got in and out, what it made or lost in dollars, how much you risked, why you took it and how you felt. A broker statement tells you *what* happened. A journal tells you *why*, and that turns a pile of results into something you can learn from: which setups pay, which mistakes cost you, and whether you're trading the plan you wrote.

## What is a trading journal for?

A journal answers three questions a P&L figure can't:

1. **Is my edge real?** Do the setups you trade actually make money over a decent number of trades, or does one lucky day hide a losing pattern?
2. **Am I following my plan?** Were the losses trades you planned, or trades you shouldn't have taken?
3. **What is costing me the most?** Moving stops, trading the open, oversizing after a loss: a journal puts a number on each one.

Without a record, you're relying on memory, and memory is kind to winners and vague about losers. Writing each trade down when it happens takes hindsight out of it.

## What should you record for each trade?

Think of it in four layers. The first can usually be imported from your platform; the other three only you can add.

### 1. The facts

- Date and time of entry and exit
- Market or contract (for example MNQ, ES or EURUSD)
- Long or short, and size (contracts or lots)
- Entry price, exit price, fees, and the P&L

### 2. The plan

- **Your initial stop.** It defines your risk on the trade.
- Your risk in dollars
- Your target, if you had one
- The setup's name, from a short list of setups you trade on purpose

### 3. The process

- A photo of the trade, so you can see what you saw at the time
- A checklist of the setup's rules, ticked or not
- An on plan or off plan mark, and a grade if you use one
- Any mistakes, named consistently (for example "moved stop", "early exit", "no setup")

### 4. The context

- How you felt before and during the trade: calm, rushed, frustrated, fearful of missing out
- What else was going on: a news release, the first minutes of the session, a losing streak earlier in the day
- One line on what you'd do again or differently

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Net P&L | +$297.90 | The dollars the trade actually made or lost |
| Initial stop | 20,980.00 | Shows the dollars at risk |
| Setup | Opening range break | Lets you see which setups pay |
| Photo | Chart at entry | Shows what you saw, not what you remember |
| Feeling | Frustrated after a loss | Reveals patterns like revenge trading |
| Note | "Entered before the candle closed" | The lesson, in your own words |

## Why start with dollars?

Dollars are what reach your account. One total P&L by day, week and month shows what you made or lost, after fees, with nothing to translate. Start there.

Some traders also measure in R. An R multiple is a trade's result divided by what you planned to risk. If you risk $200 and make $500, that's +2.5R; if you're stopped out as planned, it's −1R. R is an optional second view that puts every trade on the same scale whatever the contract or size.

Our guide on [how to calculate R multiple](/guides/how-to-calculate-r-multiple/) walks through the formula with futures examples.

## Why track setups and feelings as well as P&L?

Because the outcome of a single trade is partly luck. A trade that followed your plan and lost can still be a good trade; one that ignored your plan and won can be a habit that costs you later. The dollars tell you what happened, and the rest tells you how.

Setups and feelings are also where most patterns hide. Once you have a few weeks of data you can ask questions like:

- What do I make or lose in dollars on plan, against off plan?
- How do trades taken straight after a loss perform?
- Do I do worse in the first 15 minutes of the session?

These are questions about you, not the market, which is why a journal is useful whatever you trade.

## Why does keeping a journal work?

There's nothing magic about it. It works because it creates a feedback loop:

- **Record** each trade honestly, at the time.
- **Review** regularly, in dollars, grouped by setup and by mistake.
- **Change one thing** and measure whether it helped.

Two cautions. Small samples mislead: ten trades of a setup tell you very little, so let numbers build before you drop or double down on anything. And a journal only helps if it's complete; skipping the bad days removes exactly the trades you most need to see.

## Paper, spreadsheet or app: which should you use?

### A paper notebook

Quick to start and good for writing down reasoning and feelings in the moment. The downside is that you can't sort or total anything, so reviewing a month means reading every page.

### A spreadsheet

Free, flexible and yours to design. You can add a P&L column, filter by setup and chart your results. The cost is time: you type or paste every trade, keep the formulas working, and build each analysis yourself. Spreadsheets also struggle with fills; turning a scaled entry and two partial exits into one trade by hand is fiddly. If you go this way, our [trading journal spreadsheet template](/guides/trading-journal-spreadsheet-template/) has the columns and formulas.

### A journal app

An app imports trades from your platform, works out the statistics for you and keeps screenshots and notes with each trade. Most charge a subscription, and you should check where your data is stored and whether you can export it. Marble is free, and it reads the export from Tradovate, TopstepX, Rithmic, NinjaTrader, MetaTrader, cTrader or Interactive Brokers and pairs the fills into trades, so you only add the setup and how it felt.

Whichever you choose, the best journal is the one you'll keep up on your worst days as well as your best.

## How to start a trading journal today

1. **Write down your setups.** Two or three is plenty. For each, list the rules that must be true before you enter; see [how to write a trading setup playbook](/guides/how-to-write-a-trading-playbook/).
2. **Decide your fields.** Use the four layers above. Keep it short enough that you'll fill it in every time.
3. **Log every trade the same day,** including the ones you'd rather forget.
4. **Review once a week.** Our [weekly trade review routine](/guides/how-to-review-your-trades-weekly/) takes about 45 minutes.
5. **Change one thing at a time,** then check next week whether it worked.

If you trade a funded or evaluation account, add the firm's limits to your daily notes too; see [how to journal a prop firm evaluation](/guides/prop-firm-evaluation-journal/).
