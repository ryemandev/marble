A weekly trade review takes about 45 minutes: bring the week's trades up to date, read the numbers in dollars, find which setups paid and which mistakes cost the most, then choose **one** rule to fix next week. The aim isn't to relive every trade. It's to make one specific, measurable change at a time, and check next week whether it worked. Here's a routine you can follow every weekend.

## Before you start: complete the week's data (10 minutes)

A review is only as good as the record. First:

1. **Bring in every trade** from every account: import your platform's history, including evaluation and funded accounts.
2. **Fill the gaps.** Each trade needs its initial stop, its setup, how you felt and a short note.
3. **Mark each trade on plan or off plan,** on process, not outcome. Did it meet the setup's checklist?
4. **Add screenshots** of any trade you want to look at closely.

If you can't remember why you took a trade, that's a finding in itself: write "no reason recorded" and count it.

## Step 1: Read the week in numbers (5 minutes)

Write down:

- Number of trades and days traded
- **Net P&L** in dollars
- Win rate, average win and average loss in dollars
- **Expectancy:** average dollars per trade
- Largest loss in dollars (anything well past your planned risk is worth a look)
- Fees, as a share of gross P&L

Then compare with the last four weeks. One week is noise; the trend is what matters. If you're new to expectancy, see [what is expectancy in trading](/guides/what-is-expectancy-in-trading/). If you also measure in R, see [how to calculate R multiple](/guides/how-to-calculate-r-multiple/).

## Step 2: Which setups paid? (10 minutes)

Group the week's trades by setup. Here's an illustrative week, with example numbers:

| Setup | Trades | Win rate | Net P&L | Average trade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening range break | 9 | 44% | +$460 | +$51 |
| Pullback | 6 | 33% | −$140 | −$23 |
| No setup | 4 | 25% | −$320 | −$80 |
| **Week** | **19** | **37%** | **$0** | **$0** |

The week's total is flat, but the detail isn't. One setup made +$460; trades taken with no setup gave most of it back. Looking only at the week's total would have hidden both.

Two cautions:

- **Small samples mislead.** Six trades of a setup is too few to judge. Add the week to the setup's running record and look at that.
- **"No setup" is always a row.** Trades you can't name a setup for deserve their own line, every week. If that row is often long, your [setup playbook](/guides/how-to-write-a-trading-playbook/) may need writing down.

## Step 3: Rank your mistakes by cost (10 minutes)

Name each mistake the same way every time, so they can be counted. Then put a cost on each, in dollars:

- **Moved stop:** how much worse the trade was than your planned loss. As an example, a trade closed at −$180 against a planned −$100 cost $80.
- **No setup:** the whole result of the trade, since it shouldn't have been taken.
- **Early exit:** the dollars you'd have made holding to your planned exit, if you can tell from the chart.
- **Oversize:** the extra loss caused by the extra contracts.

| Mistake (example) | Times | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Traded with no setup | 4 | $320 |
| Moved stop | 2 | $190 |
| Early exit | 3 | $150 |

Sort by cost, not by how often it happened. A mistake you made twice can cost more than one you made five times.

Then compare your **on-plan trades** with your **off-plan trades**, in dollars. If the on-plan trades average clearly better, you've got evidence for your own plan, which helps more than willpower.

## Step 4: Check time and feelings (5 minutes)

- **By hour:** are your losses bunched at a particular time? If you trade US futures from the UK, look at the first minutes after the New York open (usually 14:30 UK) separately from the rest of the session.
- **By day:** is one weekday consistently worse?
- **By feeling:** how did trades tagged frustrated, rushed or fear of missing out perform against calm ones?
- **After a loss:** how did the next trade do? If trades straight after a loss do badly, see [how to stop revenge trading](/guides/how-to-stop-revenge-trading/).

You're looking for patterns that repeat across weeks, not one bad afternoon.

## Step 5: Look closely at two trades (5 minutes)

Pick your **best trade by process** and your **worst trade by process**, not by P&L. Replay the chart for each:

- What did you see at entry? Was it in your plan?
- Where was your stop, and why there?
- What would you do the same, and what differently?

Write one sentence for each. These are the trades worth remembering.

## Step 6: Choose one rule to fix (5 minutes)

Take the costliest mistake from Step 3 and write **one** rule that addresses it. Make it specific enough to check with a yes or no. In the example week, that might be:

> Only trade setups that are written in my plan. If I can't name the setup, I don't take the trade.

Then decide how you'll measure it next week, for example "number of no-setup trades: target zero". Put the rule somewhere you'll see it before each session.

One rule a week is enough. Changing five things at once means you'll never know which one helped.

## A weekly review template

Copy this into your notes:

1. Trades, days, net P&L, expectancy:
2. Compared with the last four weeks:
3. Best setup this week, and its running record:
4. Weakest setup, and its running record:
5. Costliest mistake, times and cost in dollars:
6. On-plan average vs off-plan average, in dollars:
7. Time or feeling pattern noticed:
8. Best trade by process, and why:
9. Worst trade by process, and why:
10. Last week's rule: kept? Did it help?
11. This week's one rule, and how I'll measure it:

If you trade a prop firm account, add each account's distance to its limits; see [how to journal a prop firm evaluation](/guides/prop-firm-evaluation-journal/).

## Common weekly review mistakes

- **Judging a setup on one week.** Use its running record.
- **Reviewing only losing trades.** Winners that broke your rules teach the most expensive lessons.
- **Skipping good weeks.** That's when bad habits are easiest to miss.
- **Too many changes.** One rule, measured, beats a list of resolutions.

## Monthly: zoom out

Once a month, run the same steps on the whole month. Check each setup's running expectancy, whether last month's rules stuck, and whether your average loss in dollars is still close to what you planned. That's where you see real trends.
